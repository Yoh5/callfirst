Why the obvious approach fails

Alerting on strangers is useless here.
She is expecting the courier.

Every doorbell on the market can tell her someone unknown is at the door. She already knows. She was told he would come, by someone she believes.

  • Call First watches for something else: a visitor nobody declared in advance, and the door opening for them.
  • Visits and deliveries are declared calmly, beforehand — a carer on Tuesdays, a parcel this afternoon. Those ring in silence.
  • Everything else is a question worth one sentence at the door.

How it works · one visit, end to end

An unexpected visitor rings

“Before you hand anything over, call your family first, and ask for your family word.”

Played by the Ring chime, in her own hallway. The family word is the one code the scammer cannot know.

The door opens anyway

Sarah is told at once — the doorbell’s still image, how sure the system is (a door sensor saw it, or only how long he stayed), and one instruction: call Margaret now. A printable report sheet for IC3 and the FTC comes with it.

For the next seven days

The family is told as soon as anyone unexpected appears — before the door opens. Couriers come back.

The threat the design names first

The victim is on the scammer’s side,
for now.

A protection the scammer can talk her out of is not a protection. So every way to weaken it is loud.

  • Pausing, switching off, adding a visit or a delivery for today, adding a new trusted person: each one tells the family first.
  • A pause ends by itself after twenty-four hours. Nothing stays off because someone forgot.
  • A fake delivery notice, written to make a courier look expected, is refused by code reading the message itself. A model may help read it. It decides nothing.

The misses are published next to the successes. Lose both doorbell webhooks for one visit and nobody is told — about one visit in a hundred, and the Partner API offers no way to ask the doorbell what it saw. A neighbour let in unannounced is a false alert.

What two days of walking the screens found

The suite proves the rules.
Walking the screens proves the product.

Five defects, and none of the 261 tests could see them — they all call the API, and all five lived in the part a person touches.

  • A required form field made the one refusal this project exists for unreachable: a collection message has no delivery window, so the browser never submitted it.
  • A question rendered below the fields it was meant to fill. The first person to use it filled them by hand and never saw it.
  • “Call Margaret now” hid behind the change alert a scammer’s next move produces a minute later.
  • A report link in the alert e-mail needed a session the phone it arrived on had never had.

What it will not do

Recognise faces. Call the police.
Let a model decide. Run without her say.

It never tries to identify a visitor. False alerts exist, so people decide. Code checks every reading against the message itself. She switches it on, chooses who is told, and one button deletes everything.

Built on the Ring Partner API. Ring publishes no simulator, so every measurement above was taken against ring-sandbox (MIT), an open source emulator written by someone else — read in full, pinned by hash, and sent one pull request back.
